* Lecture 01 - Introduction to CS 374

*Important Terms*


** Alphabets

Let \Sigma be an *alphabet*, an arbitrary *finite* set of *symbols* or *characters*

Examples include:
- \Sigma $= \{ A, B, \dots, Z \}$
- \Sigma $= \{0, 1\}$

** Strings

A *string* or *word* is a *finite* sequence of symbols from an alphabet

Formally speaking, $w$ is a string over \Sigma if it is one of
- the *empty string*
- an ordered pair $\left(a, x\right)$, where $a \in \Sigma$ and $x$ is a string over \Sigma

*** Example

\begin{align*}
 STRING &= (S, (TRING)) \\
 &= (S, (T, (RING))) \\
 &= \dots \\
 &= (S, (T, (R, (I, (N, (G, \varepsilon))))))
\end{align*}

** Functions

*Functions* are defined recursively, where length $|w|$ represents
the number of symbols in $w$

\[
\lvert w \rvert := \begin{cases}
  0 & \text{ if } w = \varepsilon \\
  1 + \lvert x \rvert & \text{ if } w = ax
\end{cases}
\]

*** Example

\begin{align*}
\lvert HAT \rvert &= 1 + \lvert AT \rvert \\
&= 1 + (1 + \lvert T \rvert) \\
&= 1 + (1 + (1 + \lvert \varepsilon \rvert)) \\
&= \boxed{3}
\end{align*}

** Concatenation

 Let *concatenation*, $w \concat z$, of strings $w$ and $z$ be represented as
 \[
    w \concat z := \begin{cases}
    z & \text{ if } w = \varepsilon \\
    (a, x \concat z) & \text{ if } w = ax
    \end{cases}
 \]

*** Example

\begin{align*}
NOW \concat HERE &= N \cdot (OW \concat HERE) \\
&= N \cdot (O \cdot (W \concat HERE)) \\
&= N \cdot (O \cdot (W \cdot (\varepsilon \concat HERE))) \\
&= N \cdot (O \cdot (W \cdot HERE)) \\
&= NOWHERE \\
\end{align*}

** Induction

- Consider an arbitrary object (probably of size $n$)
- *Explicitly* state a *strong* inductive hypothesis (i.e. ~for all k < n~
- Explicitly handle each case for the object
- Use inductive hypothesis when its useful
- End by pointing out you handled all cases

*** Example 1

For every string, $w$

\[
  w \concat \varepsilon = w
\]

Let $w$ be an arbitrary string. Assume for all string $x$, $\lvert x \rvert < \lvert w \rvert$, such that $x \concat \varepsilon = x$

Suppose $w = \varepsilon$. Then,

\begin{align*}
w \concat \varepsilon &= \varepsilon \concat \varepsilon && \text{since } w = \varepsilon \\
               &= \varepsilon              && \text{by definition of concatenation } (\concat) \\
               &= w                 && \text{since } w = \varepsilon
\end{align*}


*** Example 2

Suppose $w = a \concat x$ for symbol $a$ and string $x$. Prove that in all cases, $w \concat \varepsilon = w$

 \begin{align*}
 w \concat \varepsilon &= (a \cdot x) \concat \varepsilon && (w = ax) \\
 &= a \cdot (x \concat \varepsilon) && (\text{by definition of } \concat) \\
 &= a \cdot x && (\text{inductive hypothesis}) \\
 &= w && (w = ax)
 \end{align*}

 Therefore, in all cases, $w \concat \varepsilon = w$

*** Example 3

 Let $w$ and $z$ be arbitrary, upon the assumption that for every string $x$ such that $\lvert x \rvert < \lvert w \rvert$, that $\lvert x \concat z \rvert = \lvert x \rvert + \lvert z \rvert$

 1. Suppose that $w = \varepsilon$

    Then,
    \begin{align*}
    \lvert w \concat z \rvert &= \lvert \varepsilon \concat z \rvert && w = \varepsilon \\
    &= \lvert z \rvert && \text{by definition of concatenation } \concat \\
    &= 0 + \lvert z \rvert && \text{arithmetic} \\
    &= \lvert \varepsilon \rvert + \lvert x \rvert && \text{by definition of length of string} \\
    &= \lvert w \rvert + \lvert z \rvert && w = \varepsilon
    \end{align*}

 2. Suppose that $w = a \cdot x$ for some symbol $a$ and string $x$.

    Then,
    \begin{align*}
    \lvert w \concat z \rvert &= \lvert (a \cdot x) \concat z \rvert && w = a \cdot x \\
    &= \lvert a \cdot (x \concat z) \rvert && \text{by definition of concatenation } \concat \\
    &= 1 + \lvert x \concat z \rvert && \text{inductive hypothesis} \\
    &= 1 + \lvert x \rvert + \lvert z \rvert && \text{by definition of length of string} \\
    &= \lvert w \rvert + \lvert z \rvert && w = a \cdot x
    \end{align*}
